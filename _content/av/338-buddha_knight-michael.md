> The circle and the square represent, in Chinese art, heaven and earth. […] Almost all of these early Buddhist sculptures of this type have a square base and a circular umbrella. […] They're incorporating their own iconography into iconography that also comes from the Buddhist tradition

> The foreign rulers of this northern area were taking over a Chinese population.
They couldn't easily claim the Mandate of Heaven as they weren't Chinese.
So Buddhism was both a religion and pragmatic...

In this two-part lecture, curator Michael Knight examines the oldest (dated) Chinese Buddhist sculpture: a gilt-bronze seated Buddha from 338 CE in the Asian Art Museum.
It's a fascinating case study in how Buddhism and its culture took root in early China from the Western influence of Central Asian Buddhists from Kushan and Kucha.

He explains traditional Chinese piece-mold bronze casting and the evolving role of the human figure from Shang ritual vessels and Han money trees (*Xīwángmǔ* 西王母) to independent devotional icons such as this one.
Knight situates the 338 Buddha within the turbulent Later Zhao dynasty under the Jie rulers Shí Lè (石勒) and Shí Hǔ (石虎) and their famed Central Asian monk-advisor, Fótúdèng (佛圖澄), who he suspects may have been the model for this remarkable statue.

Originally presented by the Society for Asian Art on April 24, 2015, the lecture is now available on the Asian Art Museum's YouTube in two parts: [Part 1](https://youtu.be/JwFCUig2Lgs) and [Part 2](https://youtu.be/Vu5he7UJHh0).

You can also view the sculpture itself in the [Asian Art Museum's online collection](https://searchcollection.asianart.org/objects/2755/buddha-dated-338).
