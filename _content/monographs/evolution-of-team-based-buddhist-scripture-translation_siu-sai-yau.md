> Emperor Taizong […] believed translations should be handled by a single individual proficient in both the source and target languages, thereby minimizing inconsistencies and errors.
> However, Xuanzang […] emphasized that collaborative translation […] allowed the wider community to witness the translation process, confirming its authenticity and avoiding misunderstandings and forgeries in future generations.

> The elite translation model in the Tang Dynasty sought to integrate the best aspects of both approaches.
> It upheld the close collaboration and specialized roles found in small groups, while also embracing the exchange of ideas and inclusivity seen in large assemblies.
> This synthesis, coupled with selective participation and oversight, created an optimal structure suited for imperial patronage.

In this short, open-access monograph, Sai Yau Siu traces how medieval Chinese Buddhist scripture translation evolved from small Han-dynasty (~200 CE) partnerships to massive Later Qin (~400 CE) assemblies under Kumārajīva (鳩摩羅什) and finally into the highly structured, state-sponsored "elite translation teams" (*yìchǎng* 譯場) of the Tang Dynasty (~700 CE).
Drawing on sūtra prefaces, monastic biographies, and catalog records, the book primarily explains the division of labor inside, and politics surrounding, the major Tang bureaus led by Prabhākaramitra, Xuánzàng (玄奘), Divākara, Devaprajña, Śikṣānanda, Yìjìng (義淨), Bodhiruci, Amoghavajra (不空), and Prajña (般若).

This brief volume is largely based on the author's Chinese monograph, *Zhōnggǔ Zhōngguó Fójīng Xiézuò Fānyìshǐ* (中古中國佛經協作翻譯史) and the English sometimes smells of it.
The author uses "Theravāda" loosely to refer to the Śrāvakayāna schools as a whole, and in section 2.2.4 refers to the Liu Song dynasty (~400 CE) as the "Southern Song" Dynasty (~1200 CE), perhaps mistranslating *Nán Cháo Sòng* 南朝宋?
Despite its occasionally stilted prose, however, for those interested in how the Chinese Canon was translated, it remains a compelling read.
